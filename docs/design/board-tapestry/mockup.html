Tapestry Over Stone

Each player brings a woven playmat and lays it on a rough stone table. The cloth gives every zone a home and then stays out of the way: on a full board you see cards, not chrome.

“The present takes place in royal halls — grand with tapestry, to be sure, but that tapestry hangs over rough-hewn stone.” — world bible, The Fall

Size

Drawn live at the game’s native 960×540 and scaled up, using the real card frames and art from the prototype. Hover cards to lift them. Click the brass Pass plaque (or focus the board and press Space) to walk the turn: the day ribbon advances, and the light follows the active player.

What’s on the table

  1. 1
    Two matsEach player brings their own cloth: yours warm wool, theirs cool slate. The woven border picks up each deck’s colors.
  2. 2
    The hemLands sit in a woven band at the back of the mat as art strips (name and art). Spent lands sink and dim. Creatures still turn sideways.
  3. 3
    The fieldOpen cloth for creatures and other permanents, seven abreast. Attackers step toward the seam and blockers step out to meet them.
  4. 4
    Brass medallionLife total, struck on an old coin gone green at the rim. Artifacts in this world are older than their owners.
  5. 5
    BobbinsFloating mana, wound as thread on five spools. Empty spools stay quiet.
  6. 6
    Run tallyOne stitch per win this run. The mat travels with you from fight to fight.
  7. 7
    WellsLibrary and graveyard sit in the back corner, where they would on a paper mat. Empty wells show their embroidery: a spool, and scissors.
  8. 8
    The stackSpells are held over the seam in a cascade. The front card resolves next; a colored edge marks whose it is.
  9. 9
    The day ribbonTen knots for the ten steps, dawn to night, laid across the gap between the mats. Combat is the red stretch.
  10. 10
    Pass plaqueNames the step and what passing will do. Space does the same.
  11. 11
    ThreadsEach target is a thread in the spell’s color, tied off on what it touches. Blockers are cross-stitched to their attackers.
  12. 12
    Light poolThe active player’s side is lit. The light changes sides with the turn and warms toward dusk.
  13. 13
    HandsYours is held over the near edge of the table. Theirs shows card backs over the far edge.

How it keeps out of the way

Saturation belongs to the cards

The cloth, stone and stitching sit in a narrow, dark value range with almost no chroma. Card frames and art are the only saturated things on screen, plus the threads, which only appear when something is targeting.

The board talks in objects

Rules words stay on the cards. The board shows state with things you could put on a real table: a coin for life, spools for mana, a ribbon for the turn, a brass plate to press. The only labels are small stitched ones on empty zones, and cards cover them as the game fills up.

Every zone has one home

The two mats mirror each other. Life and the stack live in the left column, decks and Pass in the right, permanents in the middle. Your eye learns one map and uses it for both players.

The seam carries the shared things

The strip of table between the mats holds what both players share: the stack on the left, the turn ribbon through the middle, and Pass on the right. Combat happens across it, so threads have a short, clear path.

Space budget

Native canvas is 960×540, shown at 2× for 1920×1080. Cards use the prototype’s 80×112 frame, which comes out to 160×224 on screen, close to the port’s current 150×210. Heights from the top of the screen:

Across: a 120 px left column (life, stack), a 624 px field, and a 172 px right column (library, graveyard, Pass). The art strips are what make the budget work. A full-height land row would cost 58 px per side, and those pixels went to your hand and to breathing room.

Palette

The five thread colors are the house art palette from the art-language page: ivory, cerulean, wine-purple, crimson, emerald. On the board they show up only in mat borders (muted), on the bobbins, and in target threads (full strength).

Bringing it to Godot

  • Mats are one pre-rendered texture per side, regenerated only when deck colors change. Lighting is a single shader pass with an ordered dither, so it stays pixel-crisp.
  • BattlefieldZone keeps its two rows. The creature row is the field and the land row becomes the hem, drawing a cropped art-strip view of each land.
  • Pile zones become the wells. PlayerPanel becomes the plate: medallion, bobbins and tally.
  • The stack VBox turns into a cascade over the seam. It is still a plain container repainted on state_changed, not a CardContainer.
  • The phase label becomes the ribbon and the action button becomes the plaque. combat_lines.gd draws threads instead of lines.
  • Everything is laid out on a 960×540 grid at integer 2×, so pixel art never resamples unevenly.

Open questions

  • Sinking vs turning lands. A turned art strip would overflow the hem, so spent lands sink and dim. That breaks the paper “turned means tapped” habit for lands only. If it reads wrong in play, the fallback is to stack lands by type, the way most digital clients do, with a tapped count on each stack.
  • Mirror vs face-to-face. Their mat is a mirror image of yours, so life and decks line up in the same columns. A truly face-to-face table would rotate it 180°, which is more physical but puts their library on your left.
  • Where the log goes. Not on the table. A drawer that slides out from the right edge, like the prototype’s sidebar, keeps the board clean.
  • Exile. No card uses it yet. When one does, it gets a third well that only appears once something is in it.
  • Boss mats. Bosses could bring their own cloth: a different weave, a torn border, a darker light. That is cheap to make and says “this fight is different” before a card is played.
